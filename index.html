Section 1 of 6

Teaching Online

~1.5 min read

Teaching online isn't simply “the same class, but on a screen.” It asks something genuinely different of you — different tools, different rhythms, different ways of building trust and engagement with people you may never meet in person.

The good news: many of the instincts you already rely on professionally — reading a room, adapting on the fly, building credibility quickly — transfer directly, once you know where to point them.

By the end of this 30-minute session, you'll be able to:

  • Explain the key differences between in-person and online teaching, and what they mean for your planning
  • Apply practical methods and tools for engaging adult learners online
  • Explain the value of active, peer, reflective and experiential learning, and how to build them into an online class
  • Identify what makes an online presentation genuinely effective, rather than just technically functional

Throughout, you'll be asked to connect this to your own industry experience — much of what makes online teaching work is closer to how you already run remote meetings, client calls, or virtual demonstrations than you might expect.

Pitched at NFQ Level 8. The language, concepts and tasks are calibrated to honours bachelor degree standard, with comparative notes on what online teaching looks like at Level 7 and Level 9.

Section 2 of 6

In-person vs online teaching

~5 min read

Online teaching isn't a weaker version of in-person teaching — it's a different medium, with its own strengths and its own failings.

In a physical classroom, a huge amount of communication happens for free: body language, eye contact, the energy of the room, the ability to notice confusion on a face before anyone says a word. Online, almost none of that is automatic — so it has to be designed in.

Some key differences to plan around

In person Online
Social presence builds naturally through proximity
Social presence has to be deliberately built — names, cameras, check-ins
Attention is easier to hold; distractions are visible
Attention competes with email, phones and other tabs
Pace can shift instantly based on visible cues
Cues are delayed or absent — you need to actively check for understanding
One mode of delivery: you, live, in the room
Multiple modes: live sessions, recorded content, discussion boards, async tasks

The Community of Inquiry model

A useful framework here is the Community of Inquiry (CoI) model (Garrison, Anderson & Archer, 2000), which describes effective online learning as the overlap of three types of presence:

Explore · The three presences

Select each presence to see what it covers and what it takes to build it online.

Teaching presence

Your design, facilitation and direct instruction — how the session is structured, how discussion is steered, and where you step in to explain.

Online, this meansSetting a clear agenda, posting prompts that actually invite a response, and summarising where a discussion has landed before moving on.

Social presence

The sense that real people are present and engaged with one another — the part that a physical room gives you for free.

Online, this meansUsing names, encouraging cameras, opening with a low-stakes check-in, and making space for learners to respond to each other rather than only to you.

Cognitive presence

The extent to which learners can construct and confirm meaning through sustained reflection and discourse.

Online, this meansTasks that make learners test an idea, disagree with a peer, or apply a concept to their own context — not just receive an explanation.

In a physical room, social and cognitive presence often happen almost by accident. Online, all three have to be actively designed — which is really what the rest of this session is about.

A note on NFQ level and online delivery

The mode of delivery doesn't change what a module is asking students to achieve. The learning outcomes and NFQ level stay exactly the same, whether a module is taught in person or online.

What changes is how you create the conditions for students to reach them. Select a level to see how that plays out:

Ordinary Bachelor Degree

More structured, guided online tasks

Online activities often lean toward clear step-by-step exercises and closely scaffolded discussion prompts, reflecting the more defined-context expectations at this level.

Honours Bachelor Degree · this course's level

Room for independent judgement and critical exchange

Online tasks should give learners space to exercise judgement: open discussion prompts, peer critique, and case-based online work where there isn't a single tidy answer.

Master's Degree

Facilitated peer and self-directed inquiry

Online teaching shifts toward seminars, research-informed debate and collaborative knowledge-building, where your role is closer to a facilitator than an instructor.

Section 3 of 6

Engaging adult learners online: methods and tools

~7 min read

Engagement online has to be built into the structure of the session — you can't rely on the room to do it for you.

Adult learners bring experience, existing mental models, and a strong preference for relevance — a foundation well established in andragogy (Knowles, 1984) and reflected in constructivist approaches to online learning more broadly.

Online, relevance matters even more — because a disengaged adult learner can simply close the tab.

Practical methods

Five tools that do most of the work. Select each to see what it's best suited to:

Explore · Five engagement tools

Each of these does a different job. Select one to see when to reach for it.

Low-stakes polling and quick-response tools

Used at the start of a session, a poll or word cloud surfaces prior knowledge and gets everyone participating within the first two minutes.

Best forHighlighting what learners already know before you start teaching a topic.

Breakout discussions

Smaller, structured conversations are far more effective than open discussion in a large group, where only a few voices tend to dominate.

Best forGetting a small group to work through a case or problem in depth.

Chat-based contribution

Many learners who wouldn't speak up in person will type a response. Invite chat contributions explicitly — as a real input to the session, not just a backchannel.

Best forLetting quieter learners contribute without having to speak aloud.

Discussion boards & async forums

Asynchronous spaces give learners time to think before contributing, and let reflection and peer exchange continue outside live sessions.

Best forReflection and peer response over several days rather than several minutes.

Regular, brief check-ins

A simple “does this make sense so far?” every 10–15 minutes — more often than you would in person, because you can't read the room the same way.

Best forCatching confusion early, before it compounds across a whole session.

Why interaction isn't optional

Social constructivism, drawing on Vygotsky (1978), underpins much of this — the idea that learning happens most effectively through interaction and dialogue, not passive content consumption.

The practical implication An online session that's mostly you talking, for 60–90 minutes, with no structured interaction, is working against how adults actually learn — regardless of how good the content is.

Section 4 of 6

Active, peer, reflective and experiential learning online

~7 min read

Passive online content — a talking head reading slides — is the single biggest risk to learning quality in online teaching.

These four approaches are the antidote. Each one moves the learner from receiving material to doing something with it.

Four ways to make online learning active

1. Active learning

Learners doing something with the material, not just receiving it.

OnlineA live problem-solving task, an annotated document exercise, or a short applied task completed during a session rather than passively watched.

2. Peer learning

Learners teaching and challenging each other.

OnlineBreakout rooms for peer review, shared documents for collaborative work, discussion boards for peer feedback on drafts. Online tools make this genuinely easier in some ways.

3. Reflective learning

Learners making sense of their own experience against the material.

Online“Relate this concept to a professional situation you've faced, then identify what you'd do differently.”

4. Experiential learning

Learning through doing, simulation, or applied practice.

OnlineCase studies, scenario-based branching activities, virtual labs or simulations, and role-play via video breakout.

Kolb's experiential learning cycle

Reflective learning connects directly to Kolb's (2015) experiential learning cycle — a cycle that maps particularly well onto industry-experienced lecturers' own learning history, and one you can build directly into online tasks.

Why this matters at Level 8 specifically Level 8 outcomes typically require evaluation, synthesis and independent judgement — exactly the thinking these four approaches build. A recorded lecture can transmit information, but it can't develop a student's ability to critically evaluate or apply it. That requires them to actively do something with it, ideally in dialogue with others.

What makes a good online presentation

~6 min read

A good online presentation isn't about production value — it's about sustaining attention, presence and clarity in a medium that works against all three.

Six practical principles

Shorter segments, more often Attention online drops faster than in person. Aim for content chunks of 8–12 minutes maximum before a shift in activity, pace or interaction.

All cameras on, where possible — including yours Visible presence is one of the clearest, most direct ways to build the social presence element of the Community of Inquiry model.

Design for the screen, not for the room Dense slides that work on a projector often fail on a laptop screen. Favour large text, minimal text per slide, and strong visual hierarchy.

Narrate your thinking, not just your slides This is where your industry expertise genuinely shines online. Talking through why you're making a judgement, not just stating a conclusion, replicates the tacit knowledge transfer that happens naturally on the job.

Build in deliberate pauses Silence feels riskier online, but a genuine pause after a question gives learners time to unmute, type, or think. Without it, you'll answer your own questions within three seconds, every time.

Vary your delivery mode within a session Live talking head, screen share, a shared document, a poll — switching mode roughly every 10 minutes measurably helps sustain attention.

Connectivism: you don't have to be the whole network

This also connects to connectivism (Siemens, 2005; Downes, 2010) — the idea that in networked digital environments, learning is distributed across people, tools and resources, not concentrated solely in the instructor.

What this means in practice A good online presentation, particularly at Level 8, doesn't try to contain all the knowledge itself. It points learners toward relevant networks, resources and ongoing professional conversations in the field — and models how to evaluate and engage with them critically.

Section 6 of 6

Summary and reflective activity

~3.5 min read

Online teaching asks you to design deliberately for presence, engagement and active learning — things that happen almost by default in a physical room.

Let's recap

In-person vs online
The two differ most in how presence, attention and pacing are built. The Community of Inquiry model — teaching, social and cognitive presence — is a useful lens for designing online sessions. Learning outcomes and NFQ level don't change with delivery mode, but the design task does: Level 7 favours more scaffolded tasks, Level 8 independent judgement and critical exchange, Level 9 facilitated peer and self-directed inquiry.
Engaging adult learners
Relies on deliberate tools — polling, breakout discussion, chat contribution and asynchronous forums — grounded in social constructivism's emphasis on learning through interaction.
Four ways to learn
Active, peer, reflective and experiential learning counter the passivity risk of online delivery. Kolb's (2015) cycle offers a practical structure for turning your own professional experience into a genuine learning task for students.
Online presentation
About attention design, not production polish: short segments, visible presence, narrated thinking, deliberate pauses and varied delivery mode — with connectivism reminding us that learning online is distributed across people and networks, not just delivered by the lecturer.
